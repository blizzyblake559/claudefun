# Module 01 — The Machine: How Mystique Works

> *"Before any tactic, understand the machine."*

## 1. The Information Gap — The Engine of Curiosity

**The finding (Loewenstein, 1994):** Curiosity is not a passive state. It's an *aversive* state — a felt gap between what you know and what you sense you're missing. The mind is motivated to close it.

**The shape that matters:** The gap follows an **inverted-U against existing knowledge.**

```
Curiosity
    ▲
    │         ╭──────╮
    │       ╭╯        ╰╮
    │     ╭╯            ╰╮
    │   ╭╯                ╰╮
    └───────────────────────▶ How much they know about you
      (nothing)   (some)   (everything)
```

- **Too little known:** No foothold. Can't form a question. Curiosity stays flat.
- **Some known:** Partial picture, senses more. Curiosity peaks.
- **Fully known:** Nothing left to resolve. Curiosity collapses. The mind files you and moves on.

**The implication:** Mystique is not maximal concealment. A total cipher produces no curiosity — there's nothing to wonder about. Mystique is the deliberate maintenance of a **moderate, unresolved gap** — vividly known in some dimensions, deliberately open in others.

## 2. The Open Loop — Why It Persists

**The Zeigarnik tradition:** Unresolved things pull attention back. The original *memory* claim (unfinished tasks are remembered better) largely fails to replicate (Ghibellini & Meier, 2025 meta-analysis). But the *resumption* component survives: **the mind keeps returning to open loops**, re-activating an unresolved impression even without better recall.

An unresolved question about a person is a tab the mind won't close.

**What this means in practice:** You don't need to be memorable in the literal sense. You need to leave questions *open*. The person who departs a conversation with something unresolved about them will be thought about again.

## 3. Uncertainty Prolongs and Amplifies Feeling

Two findings from Wilson & Gilbert's research program:

**Finding A (Wilson et al., 2005):** Uncertainty about a *positive* event prolongs the positive mood it produces. Once you explain or resolve an experience, it stops commanding attention — the emotion dampens. Mystery extends the pleasure.

**Finding B (Whitchurch, Wilson & Gilbert, 2011):** Women uncertain whether men liked them "a lot or an average amount" were *more* attracted than women told the men liked them a lot. The mediator was **rumination** — they thought about the men more, and thinking-about drives attraction.

**The crucial boundary people get wrong:** The effect is uncertainty about *positive regard*, not feigned coldness. The operative state is *"I'm not sure where I stand, and it's pleasant."* That's magnetic. Cold disinterest is not. Playing hard to get is weak and well-documented as such. You are not trying to seem unavailable. You are leaving the *meaning* of your warmth slightly unresolved.

## 4. The Dosing Law — The Exposure Curve

**The finding:** Mere-exposure (Zajonc, 1968; Bornstein meta-analysis, 1989, r ≈ 0.26) says familiarity breeds liking. But satiation is real (Bornstein, Kale & Cornell, 1990): past a point, repeated exposure breeds boredom and declining affect.

```
Liking/Affect
    ▲
    │              ★ ← You want to live here
    │           ╭──╮
    │         ╭╯    ╰╮
    │       ╭╯        ╰──────── (boredom/contempt)
    │     ╭╯
    └─────────────────────────▶ Exposure / Presence
         (unseen)  (optimal)   (overexposed)
```

"Familiarity breeds contempt" is not contempt exactly — it's *boredom*. Your job: live at the peak. Present enough for the liking effect. Scarce enough to never trip satiation.

## 5. The Disclosure Paradox — Resolved

**The apparent contradiction:**
- Collins & Miller (1994, meta-analysis of 94 studies): self-disclosure increases liking
- Norton, Frost & Ariely (2007): more information decreases liking ("lure of ambiguity")

Both are partly contested, but the reconciliation is robust and is the most practically useful rule in this curriculum:

> **Disclose deep and narrow, not wide and shallow.**

| Type | Direction | Effect |
|------|-----------|--------|
| Intimate, reciprocal, escalating disclosure → *chosen few* | Deep + narrow | Builds genuine bonds (Collins & Miller) |
| High-volume disclosure → *wide audience* | Wide + shallow | Closes the gap, trips satiation, invites dissimilarity cascade (Norton) |

**The operating rule:** The fascinating person is not closed off — he is intensely known by five people and deliberately under-known by five hundred.

---

## The Synthesis Sentence

> **A moderate, unresolved, positively-charged knowledge gap captures and prolongs attention.**

- Loewenstein gives the *appetite*
- The open loop gives the *persistence*
- Uncertainty gives the *pleasure and duration*
- The exposure curve gives the *dosing*
- The disclosure rule governs *who gets which version of you*

Everything else in this curriculum is an application of that sentence.
