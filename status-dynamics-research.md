## 1. How Status Actually Works

### Status is a need, not a vanity

Start with the uncomfortable part: caring about status is not a character flaw you can think your way out of. A major review of the empirical literature (Anderson, Hildreth & Howland, 2015, *Psychological Bulletin*) concluded that the desire for status — the respect, admiration, and voluntary deference of others — is a **fundamental human motive**, observed across cultures, ages, genders, and personality types, and explicitly distinct from the desires for money, power, or mere belonging. People monitor status dynamics vigilantly, work to appear socially valuable, and react strongly when their standing is threatened. Your well-being, self-esteem, and even physical health track the status others grant you.

Will Storr puts it bluntly in *The Status Game* (2021): "To our brains, status is a resource as real as oxygen or water." When you were pushed out of your old circle, the pain you felt wasn't weakness — it was the system working as designed. Mark Leary's **sociometer theory** (Leary, 1999, 2005) holds that self-esteem itself is essentially a gauge: an internal meter that tracks your relational value to others and drops sharply to warn you of impending social devaluation. The crash you feel after exclusion is an alarm, not a verdict.

### Status is conferred, never claimed

This is the single most important mechanical fact about status, and almost everything in this manual flows from it. Sociologist Cecilia Ridgeway (*Status*, 2019) identifies status as a third fundamental social resource alongside money and power — but unlike money and power, status consists of **esteem granted by others, based on shared beliefs about who and what is valuable**. You cannot award it to yourself. Announcing your own importance doesn't register as status; it registers as a *claim*, and claims trigger audit. Worse, research shows that people who are *seen to be seeking* status are granted less of it (Mahadevan et al., 2024). The harder you visibly reach, the further it recedes.

Practical consequence: every status strategy in this manual is really a strategy for changing **what other people conclude on their own**. You manage the evidence; they issue the verdict.

### Two roads up: dominance and prestige

The best-supported model of how humans actually rise is the **dominance–prestige framework** (Cheng, Tracy, Foulsham, Kingstone & Henrich, 2013, *JPSP* — "Two Ways to the Top"):

- **Dominance**: rank acquired by inducing *fear* — intimidation, coercion, control of costs. It works. In group experiments, dominant individuals gained real influence and drew more visual attention from others.
- **Prestige**: rank acquired by earning *respect* — demonstrated skill, knowledge, and generosity that others want access to. It also works, and equally well.

The critical difference: prestigious individuals were **liked**; dominant individuals were not. Both got rank, but only prestige got rank plus affection. In a friend group — where people can leave freely and there's no payroll holding them in place — dominance is structurally fragile: the moment people have an exit, fear-based rank evaporates. Prestige is the only currency that compounds in voluntary relationships. (Note: the claim that prestige is more *durable* is a reasonable inference from these findings, not a directly measured longitudinal result — see §10.)

Storr's three status games map onto this: **dominance games** (rank through force), **virtue games** (rank through conspicuous moral conformity), and **success games** (rank through competence and achievement). Friend groups run all three simultaneously. Knowing which game your group is actually scoring is half the battle — a group that talks success but scores virtue will punish your achievements and reward your conformity, and you'll be confused about why your wins don't land.

### Status is local

This is the finding that should reorganize how you think about your old circle. The **local-ladder effect** (Anderson, Kraus, Galinsky & Keltner, 2012, *Psychological Science*): respect and admiration within your *face-to-face groups* predicts well-being **more strongly than socioeconomic status does**. Money in the abstract does less for you than being genuinely esteemed by the fifteen people you actually see. The companion finding is the **big-fish–little-pond effect** (Davis, 1966; Marsh and colleagues): your felt status is computed relative to your immediate pond, not the world. Equally capable people feel small in a high-status pond and large in a modest one.

Two consequences:

1. **You can change your status overnight by changing ponds.** Status is not a property of you; it's a property of the relationship between you and a specific audience. The man rated lowest in one room is rated highest in another without changing anything about himself.
2. **Local hierarchies form fast.** Groups differentiate into rank order **within minutes** of first interaction, mainly through who participates, who appears competent, and who seems committed to the group (Anderson & Kilduff, 2009). First impressions in a new circle are not a soft factor; they are the founding constitution.

### The hardware underneath

Briefly, because it disciplines expectations: status physiology is real but messier than the popular versions. The **dual-hormone hypothesis** (Mehta & Josephs, 2010) found testosterone predicts dominance behavior only when cortisol is low — chronic stress literally blocks the drive to compete for rank. (The popular "high serotonin = high status" lobster story is contested and oversimplified in humans; treat it as a meme, not a mechanism — see §10.) The actionable residue: a stressed, anxious operator cannot run status behavior convincingly. Managing your baseline state — sleep, training, downregulating chronic threat — is upstream of every tactic below.

## 2. Principles

These are the durable laws. Tactics change with context; these don't.

**Principle 1 — Status is granted by the audience, so play to the audit, not the applause.**
(Ridgeway, 2019; Mahadevan et al., 2024.) Everything you do is evidence in a trial you don't preside over. Self-promotion is testimony from an interested witness — heavily discounted. Demonstration, third-party endorsement, and observable behavior are physical evidence. Always prefer evidence over testimony.

**Principle 2 — Visible pursuit lowers the prize.**
People perceived to *desire* status are afforded less of it (Mahadevan et al., 2024), and ostracized people who chase re-inclusion read as lower value (see §6). Oren Klaff's formulation from the deal world is identical: "Neediness, otherwise known as validation-seeking behavior, is a sign of weakness" (*Pitch Anything*, 2011). The status game is one of the few games you win partly by appearing not to be playing it.

**Principle 3 — Prestige beats dominance in any group people can leave.**
(Cheng et al., 2013.) Friend groups are voluntary associations. Fear-based rank requires captive subjects. Build rank on being someone people *want* access to — skill, judgment, generosity, good rooms — not someone they're afraid to cross.

**Principle 4 — Generosity is a status investment, not a status cost.**
The **competitive altruism** studies (Hardy & Van Vugt, 2006, "Nice Guys Finish First") found that the most publicly generous group members earned the *highest* status and were most preferred as partners — and the costlier the generosity, the larger the status return. Giving, hosting, connecting, and teaching are not what you do *after* you have status. They are how status is manufactured.

**Principle 5 — Scarcity prices everything, including you.**
Scarce things are valued more (Worchel, Lee & Adewole, 1975 — the cookie-jar experiment: identical cookies rated more desirable when only two remained, especially when scarcity was driven by demand). Robert Greene's Law 16 — "Use absence to increase respect and honor" — is this principle applied to the self. Total availability signals that your time clears the market at zero. (Note: "scarcity of presence" as a *status* signal specifically is an extrapolation from scarcity and costly-signaling theory plus practitioner consensus, not a directly tested finding — see §10. It is, however, one of the safest extrapolations in this manual.)

**Principle 6 — Status is something you do, continuously.**
Keith Johnstone's discovery in *Impro* (1979): status is not a rank you hold but a behavior you transact, moment to moment — "every inflection and movement implies a status," and the transactions never pause, even (especially) among friends. This is bad news and good news. Bad: you are always playing, including when you think you're off-duty. Good: because status is performed, it is *trainable* — and small mechanical changes (§3) move the needle immediately.

**Principle 7 — Hold the frame; whoever needs to explain themselves has lost it.**
Klaff's collision rule: when two frames meet, only one survives, and "if you have to explain your authority, power, position, leverage, and advantage, you do not hold the stronger frame" (*Pitch Anything*, 2011). Justifying, qualifying, and over-explaining are the universal tells of the weaker frame. Full treatment in §5.

**Principle 8 — Rising provokes envy; manage it deliberately or it manages you.**
A similar peer who surpasses us is the most threatening comparison there is (Festinger, 1954; Tesser's self-evaluation maintenance model, 1988). Envy splits into **benign** (motivates the envier to climb) and **malicious** (motivates the envier to pull you down) (van de Ven, Zeelenberg & Pieters, 2009). High achievers attract cut-down attempts — the "tall poppy" effect is empirically real: people report *pleasure* at a high achiever's fall (Feather, 1989). You cannot opt out of being envied once you rise; you can only steer envy toward its benign form (§6, §9).

**Principle 9 — Local status beats global status; choose your pond deliberately.**
(Anderson et al., 2012; big-fish–little-pond research.) The respect of your actual circle does more for you than abstract rank. Corollary: when a pond refuses to value you, the highest-percentage move is usually not to fight the pond's pricing — it's to relist in a market that bids.

**Principle 10 — Warmth and assertiveness multiply; either alone fails.**
Alison Fragale's research synthesis (*Likeable Badass*, 2024): status is granted to people judged both **caring** (warm, on our side) and **capable** (assertive, competent). Pure assertiveness reads as threat; pure warmth reads as harmless. The matrix matters because most people fail by over-rotating to one axis — the try-hard who is all capability and no warmth, or the pleaser who is all warmth and no edge.

## 3. Tactics — How to Speak and How to Act

Status is read off your body and voice long before anyone evaluates your accomplishments. Johnstone trained actors to flip audience perception of the same person within seconds using only these levers. They are the highest-leverage, fastest-feedback tactics in this manual.

### 3.1 The Johnstone behavioral inventory

From the Status chapter of *Impro* (1979), corroborated by decades of improv pedagogy:

**High-status behaviors** — stillness; an erect, open posture that takes its space; initiating and holding eye contact; a level, unhurried voice; **complete sentences without self-interruption**; keeping the head still while speaking; comfort with silence; slow, economical movement.

**Low-status behaviors** — fidgeting; touching your face or hair while talking; quick glances away after making eye contact; hunched shoulders and minimized space; hesitation fillers ("er," "um") before sentences; self-interrupting and trailing off; nervous laughter; rushing.

Johnstone's deepest observation is that these are *causal levers*, not just symptoms — he could lower an actor's status by instructing them to add one "er" at the start of each sentence, and raise it by having them hold their head still while speaking. Pick **one** mechanical change at a time and run it for two weeks. The two with the best effort-to-effect ratio: kill the pre-sentence filler, and stop touching your face when you talk.

Two refinements from Johnstone worth keeping:

- **The see-saw.** "I go up and you go down." Most casual banter is status movement — teasing lowers the target, self-deprecation lowers the self, compliments raise the other. Once you see the see-saw you can stop riding it unconsciously. The strongest social position is the one that can *raise others without falling* — confer status freely from a stable platform (this is prestige behavior, and it's what Hardy & Van Vugt's competitive-altruism findings predict will elevate you).
- **Play one step up, not ten.** Johnstone taught actors to play status slightly above or below their partner, not at extremes. Maximum-status behavior in a friend group reads as domineering and triggers coalition pushback. One notch of calm elevation is uncontestable; a throne demands a siege.

### 3.2 Speech: powerful vs. powerless style

The courtroom studies of Erickson, Lind, Johnson & O'Barr (1978) manipulated nothing but speech style and measured judgments of the same testimony. The **powerless style** — hedges ("sort of," "I think maybe"), hesitation forms ("uh," "well"), tag questions ("right?"), excessive politeness, intensifiers ("really, really") — cratered perceived credibility, competence, and attractiveness. The **powerful style** — the same content with those markers stripped out, in shorter declarative sentences — raised all three. Bradac and colleagues replicated the pattern through the 1980s.

Operational rules:

1. **Declare, then stop.** "I'm not coming Friday" outranks "I was kind of thinking I might skip Friday, if that's okay?" The first is information; the second is a request for permission to have a preference.
2. **Say less than necessary** (Greene, Law 4 — one of the few of his laws that is independently supported, via the powerless-speech literature). Over-explanation is hedging at paragraph scale. Every unrequested justification you attach to a decision advertises that you expect to be challenged.
3. **Let silence sit.** Low status rushes to fill pauses; high status lets the other party feel the weight of them. In negotiation and in banter alike, the person uncomfortable with silence concedes first.
4. **Vocal settle.** Joey Cheng's vocal-pitch work (Cheng et al., 2016, *JEP: General*) found that people whose pitch *dropped* over their first few utterances in a new group were rated more dominant and actually rose in rank; rising pitch predicted falling rank. Don't perform a fake bass — just slow down and settle. Pitch follows calm.

Caveat for friend groups: the powerful style is a tool, not a personality. Run it at full courtroom strength over beers and you'll read as cold. Fragale's warmth-plus-assertiveness finding is the governor: keep the declarative spine, add warmth through attention, humor, and generosity — not through hedging.

### 3.3 The body: attention, eyes, reactivity

- **Attention flows up the hierarchy.** Michael Chance's attention-structure work (Chance, 1967) established, across primate species and humans, that subordinates monitor dominants far more than the reverse. The group's rank order *is* its attention structure. Read any room with this: status belongs to whoever is being tracked, not whoever is loudest. And manage your own attention accordingly — the person who visibly orbits the group's center, checking for reactions after every joke, is broadcasting subordination. (The stronger pop version — "high status reacts slower" as a specific latency finding — is not directly verified; treat non-reactivity as an inference from attention structure and the composure literature, see §10.)
- **Visual dominance ratio** (Exline, Ellyson and colleagues, 1975–1988): high-power individuals look at others *while speaking* roughly as much as while listening; low-power individuals look away while speaking and gaze attentively while listening. Hold eye contact while you talk. When listening, attend warmly but don't gaze up at the speaker like a press conference.
- **Composure under provocation.** The behavioral signature that most reliably separates rank in mixed evidence — Johnstone's stillness, the interruption literature (higher-status people are interrupted less and recover the floor calmly), Klaff's croc-brain model — is *non-reactivity to small provocations*. When teased, the low-status move is to flinch, over-laugh, or counter-attack hot. The high-status move is the unhurried half-smile and a relaxed response on your own timing. You don't ignore the ball; you decide when to throw it back.
- **Power posing — mostly dead.** The famous claim that two minutes of expansive posture changes your hormones and risk behavior failed replication, and first author Dana Carney publicly disavowed the effect in 2016. The surviving residue is narrow: expansive posture may modestly increase *felt* confidence (Cuddy, Schultz & Fosse, 2018), and *observers* do read expansive, still postures as higher status (that part was never in dispute — it's Johnstone's territory). Pose for the audience, not your endocrine system.

### 3.4 Raising status inside an existing group vs. switching games

**Inside an existing group**, your status is anchored by precedent — the group "knows" your rank, and every member's self-evaluation is partly invested in you staying there (Tesser's SEM model, §6). Rises from within are slow and must be *deniable*: gradual upgrades in competence, hosting, and composure that never force the group to formally re-vote your rank. The moves that work are prestige moves — become the one who organizes the trip, who knows the guy, whose judgment gets quoted — because they raise you while delivering value, giving the group a selfish reason to ratify your rise (Anderson & Kilduff, 2009: status is pursued successfully through apparent competence, generosity, and commitment).

**Switching games** is faster and is systematically underrated because of sunk costs (§6.5). New groups rank you within minutes on present behavior, not your history (Anderson & Kilduff, 2009). Everything in §3.1–3.3 compounds in a fresh pond where no one holds a precedent file on you. The deep strategic insight from the big-fish–little-pond literature: people fight for years to move one rank in a hostile hierarchy when the same energy would buy top-three standing in a better-chosen one.

## 4. Psychological Triggers

These are the levers that move other people's status *perception* of you. They run on Cialdini's influence principles (*Influence*, 1984; expanded 2021) and the attraction/audience literature. Triggers are amplifiers: they multiply real value and they eventually expose fake value. Build the asset, then signal it.

**Trigger 1 — Social proof.** In uncertainty, people decide what you're worth by watching what others seem to think you're worth (Cialdini; Goldstein, Cialdini & Griskevicius, 2008 — though note the towel-study replication wobble, §10). Status is the ultimate ambiguous quantity, so it runs almost entirely on social proof. Practically: arrive *with* people, be *introduced* rather than introducing yourself when possible, and let evidence of other people valuing you (invitations, requests for your judgment, friends who speak well of you in your absence) circulate on its own. One warm third-party endorsement outweighs an hour of self-report.

**Trigger 2 — Preselection.** The sharpened special case of social proof: being visibly valued by desirable others. In mate-choice-copying studies, women rated the same man as more desirable when shown surrounded by interested women than when alone (Hill & Buss, 2008; Dunn & Doria, 2010 — effects are real in aggregate but inconsistent study-to-study, see §10). The general social form: a person already embedded in a warm, high-quality circle is pre-vetted; a person with no observable circle is an unpriced asset everyone discounts. This is a structural argument for §7 — your inner circle is not just companionship, it's your walking proof-of-value.

**Trigger 3 — Scarcity.** (Worchel et al., 1975.) Value tracks restricted supply, and the cookie study's sharpest detail is that scarcity *caused by demand* raised value most. Applied: limited availability reads highest-status when it's visibly because your time is claimed by things and people that matter, not because you're sulking. Decline warmly, occasionally, and without explanation: "Can't this time — have a great one." The absence of a justification is the signal.

**Trigger 4 — Intrigue.** The croc brain ignores the familiar and locks onto the novel and unresolved (Klaff, 2011). Socially: the person who is entirely legible — whose whole life is narrated on demand and on social media — has no gradient left for curiosity to climb. Keep parts of your life undisclosed not as secrecy but as inventory. Answer some questions with less than everything. Mystery is cheap to maintain and compounds (Greene's Law 6 gets the goal right — attention — but court it through intrigue, not noise).

**Trigger 5 — Authority (competence display).** People defer to demonstrated expertise (Cialdini; the Milgram lineage, with its caveats — §10). The social-circle version is Klaff's **flash roll** (*Flip the Script*, 2019): sixty to ninety seconds of fluent, casual, technically dense mastery of your domain, delivered as if it's nothing special, then dropped. Fluency plus nonchalance is the formula; either alone fails (fluency with eagerness reads as performing; nonchalance without fluency is just silence).

**Trigger 6 — Reciprocity and the Ben Franklin effect.** Favors bind in both directions. Receiving creates obligation (Regan, 1971), but the stranger lever is that *granting someone the chance to do you a small favor makes them like you more* (Jecker & Landy, 1969). People justify their effort by upgrading their opinion of its recipient. Asking a high-quality person for a small, specific, low-cost favor — advice, a book recommendation, an introduction — is simultaneously a bonding move and a status-neutral one, *provided it stays small and you repay asymmetrically* (§7).

**Trigger 7 — The audience effect.** People are more generous, more prosocial, and more status-conscious when third parties are watching, because reputation is being written (reputation-management literature; Iredale, Van Vugt & Dunbar's "altruism as courtship display"). Two applications: (a) public generosity earns status that private generosity doesn't — competitive altruism needs an audience to pay out (Hardy & Van Vugt, 2006); (b) every conflict changes character the moment it has spectators — never force a status concession from someone in front of their audience unless you intend to make a permanent enemy, and never offer one in front of yours.

**Trigger 8 — Unity.** Cialdini's seventh principle (2021): shared identity — "one of us" — moves people beyond mere liking. Klaff's **status tip-off** is the deal-room version: an early, casual signal that you belong to the other person's tribe ("I get you; I'm like you"). In social life this is the disproportionate power of shared niche history, shared craft, shared place. Lead with the overlap.

## 5. Frame Control — Klaff, Translated to Social Life

Oren Klaff (*Pitch Anything*, 2011; *Flip the Script*, 2019) built his system for high-stakes capital raising, but its chassis is general-purpose status mechanics. Two ideas underlie everything:

**The croc brain filter.** Your message is composed in your reasoning brain but *received* by the other person's primitive one, which filters by simple rules: ignore the non-novel, summarize and discard detail, treat complexity as threat. Hence: be simple, be vivid, be novel, be unbothered. Long explanations die in the filter — which is one more reason over-explaining is low status.

**Frames and the collision rule.** A frame is "the instrument you use to package your power, authority, strength, information, and status" — the unstated answer to *who is evaluating whom here?* "Frames do not coexist in the same time and place for long... one or the other gains control. Only one frame survives." And the tell of the loser: **if you have to explain your authority, you do not hold the stronger frame.**

Frames are never announced. They're carried in posture, timing, and what each party treats as needing justification. Every social encounter settles one silently.

### 5.1 The four frame attacks and their counters

**The power frame** — carried by the person used to deference: the group's self-appointed center, the rich friend, the old gatekeeper. It assumes your role is to seek approval. *Counter: the power-busting frame* — a small act of defiance or denial, **"mildly shocking but not unfriendly,"** wrapped in humor. Klaff's example: drawing on a napkin during a pitch, then snatching it back when the executive reaches for it — "you can't have this yet." The social translation is the warm tease delivered upward: playful non-compliance that demonstrates, without aggression, that you don't operate inside their approval economy. Calibration is everything — defiance without humor is just rudeness, and it triggers the other side's croc-brain defenses. The spirit is a grin, not a snarl.

**The time frame** — control asserted through the clock: the friend who's always "got five minutes," who keeps you waiting, who decides when things end. Whoever owns the clock owns the rank. *Counter: the time-constraining frame* — own your own clock. Have a real end time and honor it; be the one who leaves the gathering while it's still good rather than the last one orbiting; when someone keeps you waiting past reason, leave pleasantly ("ran out of time — next week?"). Klaff's rule for a decision-maker fifteen minutes late was to politely walk. The social form is identical: your time behaves like the time of someone whose time is claimed.

**The analyst frame** — the interrogation posture: skeptical cross-examination designed to put you on the witness stand ("So what exactly is your plan? How much does that even pay? And what happens if it doesn't work?"). Answer item-by-item and you've accepted the role of defendant. *Counter: the intrigue frame* — a brief, personal, tension-loaded story with the ending withheld. Klaff's mechanism: brains can't run cold analysis and hot narrative at the same time; the story yanks the exchange out of audit mode. Socially: respond to the audit with the *interesting* version, not the defensive version — two sentences of genuine stakes ("Funny you ask — the whole thing nearly died last month over one phone call...") — and let them pull for the rest. You've flipped from examined to narrator, which is to say from low chair to high one.

**The prize frame** — the master frame; the other three are special cases. At any moment, one party is implicitly the prize being competed for and the other is the commodity competing. "Prizing is the sum of the actions you take to get your target to understand that he is a commodity and you are the prize" (Klaff, 2011). Most people who get gatekept have silently accepted commodity position — auditioning for re-admission to a group that has appointed itself the prize. The reversal is mostly internal: genuinely hold the question *"why do I want these people in my life?"* — Klaff's qualifying question turned to social use — and let your behavior follow from it. People read which side of that question you're living on with high accuracy. Prizing fails as a bluff and works automatically once it's true, which is why §7 (building real alternatives) is the engine and this frame is just the transmission.

**The moral-authority frame** — Klaff calls the doctor's frame "may be the most powerful moral authority frame in the world": the President commands armies, but undresses when his doctor tells him to. Rank yields to *legitimate care plus expertise*. The social translation: standing that comes from being genuinely *for* people — the friend whose advice is trusted because it has no angle — outranks every wealth and dominance display in the room. It cannot be faked for long, and it is the frame most worth actually earning. (Klaff's airport case study won against spreadsheet-wielding competitors by framing the bid around heritage and community — values beat numbers when the values are credible.)

### 5.2 Holding frame under pressure

1. **Don't defend; redirect.** The instant you justify yourself at length, the collision is over (Principle 7). Respond to frame attacks with warmth, brevity, humor, or a question — anything but a defense brief.
2. **Amused, not aroused.** The croc-brain read of a person under social attack is binary: rattled or unbothered. The half-beat pause and the relaxed reply do more than any clever line. Wit helps; calm is mandatory.
3. **Eradicate visible neediness.** Klaff's three-step (via his "Tao of Steve" borrowing): want nothing visibly; be excellent in front of people; withdraw at the moment of maximum interest. Socially: bring value, enjoy yourself, leave a little early. The compulsions to over-stay, over-text, over-follow-up are the neediness leaks that undo everything else.
4. **Avoid beta traps.** Klaff's term for rituals that pre-stage you as subordinate — the lobby wait, the visitor badge. Friend groups have them too: the perpetual guest who never hosts, the one who waits to be invited, always traveling to their territory on their schedule. You don't defeat beta traps by complaining about them; you defeat them by building venues where you set the defaults (§7).
5. **Local star power.** "As soon as you take power, quickly move the discussion into an area where you are the domain expert" (Klaff, 2011). Status is situational — his example is the French waiter who outranks the executive *inside the restaurant* by controlling the sequence. You always have a domain. Steer encounters toward it, and when others hold the floor in *their* domain, defer graciously — situational deference to real expertise is itself high-prestige behavior.
6. **Inception over insistence** (*Flip the Script*, 2019): people trust their own ideas and resist yours, so the highest form of frame control is arranging the evidence and letting the other person originate the conclusion — including the conclusion that you've risen. You don't announce the new you to old friends (a claim, instantly audited); you let them *discover* it and say it first.

**The honest caveat on Klaff.** Critics correctly note his system is one-dimensional — frame *dominance*, built for one-shot, high-stakes pitches — and that constant power-taking "leads to an escalation of conflicts and can ruin relationships" (The Power Moves review). Klaff himself softened from dominance (*Pitch Anything*) to alignment and inception (*Flip the Script*). Use frame control in social life the way you use a parking brake: engaged against people running games on you, released around people who aren't. Running power-busting disrupters on a friend who isn't frame-attacking you isn't alpha; it's exhausting, and voluntary associates will quietly vote with their feet.

## 6. The Gatekeeper Playbook

The specific scenario: long-time close associates who control access to the circle you helped build, who gatekeep it, and who pushed you out. This section runs diagnosis → mistakes → repositioning → re-encounter scripts → the elevate-or-exit decision.

### 6.1 Diagnosis: why old friends gatekeep risers

Hold four findings together and the behavior stops being mysterious:

**1. Threatened dominators divide and exclude — and target the talented.** Case & Maner (2014, *JPSP*, "Divide and Conquer"): across four experiments, leaders high in *dominance* motivation (not prestige motivation), **when their power felt threatened**, deliberately restricted communication among group members, physically sequestered them, and **prevented skilled members from bonding with each other** — and aimed these moves specifically at the *most talented, most threatening* members. When their position was made secure, the behavior vanished. Read that twice: in a dominance-driven gatekeeper, **exclusion is a tax on perceived threat**. Being frozen out by an old guard is, perversely, often evidence that you had risen enough to register.

**2. Close + similar + same domain = maximum threat.** Tesser's **self-evaluation maintenance model** (Tesser & Campbell, 1982; Tesser, 1988): when someone *close to us* outperforms us in a domain *central to our own self-definition*, it's a direct hit to self-evaluation — and people respond by distancing, derogating, or quietly hindering the riser. (When the domain is irrelevant to their identity, they bask in your glory instead.) This is why your oldest associates — same domain, same starting line, maximum closeness — are structurally the *worst-positioned* people on earth to enjoy your rise, and why strangers often celebrate you more easily than your origin group does. Their gatekeeping usually isn't a judgment of your worth; it's protection of theirs.

**3. Envy comes in two flavors, and you were dealt the bad one.** Benign envy motivates leveling *up*; malicious envy motivates pulling *down* (van de Ven et al., 2009). The tall-poppy finding (Feather, 1989) adds that people feel genuine pleasure at a high achiever's fall. A circle that pushed you out as you grew was running malicious-envy dynamics, likely administered by its most dominance-motivated member with the others conflict-averse and defaulting to the incumbent.

**4. Your pain is hardware, not weakness.** Ostracism activates pain-linked brain circuitry (Eisenberger, Lieberman & Williams, 2003, *Science*), and the effect is large and brutally robust — a meta-analysis of 120 studies, d > 1.4 (Hartgerink et al., 2015). Williams' **temporal need-threat model** (2009) says exclusion strikes four needs at once — belonging, self-esteem, control, meaningful existence — and proceeds in stages: a **reflexive** stage (raw, automatic pain — felt even when excluded by a *despised* group; Gonsalkorale & Williams, 2007, the "KKK won't let me play" study), a **reflective** stage (coping and sense-making), and, if exclusion grinds on unresolved, a **resignation** stage of alienation and depression (Riva et al., 2017). Strategy lives in the reflective stage. The whole purpose of this playbook is to run the reflective stage well and never reach resignation.

### 6.2 The mistakes that finish the job for them

Ostracism research shows excluded people automatically become hyper-attentive to social cues and unconsciously *mimic* others more, an automatic re-affiliation reflex (Lakin, Chartrand & Arkin, 2008). The reflex is aimed at exactly the wrong target. The canonical errors:

- **Seeking re-entry.** Pleading, circling, engineering run-ins, asking mutuals to advocate. Two findings converge against it: visible desire for status lowers the status granted (Mahadevan et al., 2024), and the post-exclusion drive to reconnect produces good outcomes with *new* people, not with perpetrators (Maner, DeWall, Baumeister & Schaller, 2007 — the reconnection motive specifically did not extend to those who did the excluding). Chasing re-entry confirms the gatekeeper's frame: they are the prize, you are the applicant. It is the lowest-percentage move available.
- **Justifying.** Sending the long message that explains your side. Principle 7: the one explaining holds the weaker frame. To a dominance-motivated gatekeeper, your justification isn't information — it's confirmation of control, forwarded as evidence.
- **Complaining to the group.** Badmouthing the gatekeeper to remaining members forces a public loyalty vote while the incumbent holds the territory and you hold the grievance. Incumbents win those votes. It also makes you the unpleasant topic — Greene's Law 10 (avoid the perpetually aggrieved) operating against you. Even justified bitterness reads, at croc-brain level, as low status.
- **The hot dramatic exit.** Burning everything down in a final message. Exclusion does push some people to aggression (the other branch of Williams' reflective stage), and it feels like potency. It hands the gatekeeper their retroactive justification: *see — this is why.*
- **Staying past the verdict.** Hanging around the periphery accepting downgraded terms because of history. That's sunk cost (§6.5), and chronic ostracism is precisely the input that produces the resignation stage.

### 6.3 Repositioning

The strategic reframe, in one line: **stop trying to win re-admission; start making the exclusion expensive.**

1. **Full, clean, warm withdrawal.** No announcement (announcements are claims; they invite audit). Stop attending, stop reaching out, stop monitoring — *including their social media*, which keeps the wound and the attention structure pointed the wrong way (attention flows up; aim yours elsewhere). Stay impeccably pleasant in incidental contact. This is Greene's Law 36 — "disdain things you cannot have; ignoring them is the best revenge" — but the mechanism isn't spite: indifference is the only response a dominance-play can't metabolize. Punishment rewards them with evidence of impact; pleading rewards them with rank. Genuine, visible *fine-ness* starves the move entirely.
2. **Redirect the reconnection drive at new ties.** This is exactly what works in the data: excluded people who channel the affiliation motive toward new people form bonds readily (Maner et al., 2007). Caveat from the same research: high fear of negative evaluation suppresses the affiliative response — if the exclusion left you flinchy, start with low-stakes structured contact (a class, a gym, a recurring table) where rejection risk is near zero, and rebuild outward. Section 7 is the full build-out.
3. **Compound in your domain.** The gatekeeping was a bet that your trajectory could be capped by access denial. The only rebuttal that matters is the trajectory continuing without the access. Quiet competence-compounding also generates the third-party social proof (§4) that will reach the old circle without you sending it — and inception (§5.2) does the rest: let them originate the conclusion that pushing you out was the mistake.
4. **Keep honest books on the mutuals.** Members who stayed passive aren't all enemies; conflict-averse people default to incumbents. Maintain warm, individual, *off-territory* lines to the ones you actually value — coffee on neutral ground, no group talk. This quietly demonstrates that the gatekeeper's control of the *venue* was never control of *you*, and it routes around exactly the bond-blocking behavior Case & Maner documented. The gatekeeper's power was always the routing table; build direct routes.

### 6.4 Re-encounter scripts

You will run into them. The objective in every script is identical: warm, brief, unbothered, slightly scarce — and you end the exchange. (Per §3: head still, no fillers, declaratives, comfortable silence. Per §5: no explaining.)

- **The casual collision.** Warm smile, genuine for three minutes — *"Hey! Good to see you, man. How've you been?"* — fully present, zero history, then you close it: *"Good seeing you — I've got to run."* You were pleasant; you were brief; you left first. Time frame and prize frame in nine words.
- **The probe** (*"So what are you even up to these days?"* with audit energy — an analyst-frame attack). Don't file the defensive report. Intrigue frame, two sentences, ending withheld: *"Honestly, kind of a wild year — there's a thing I'm in the middle of that I can't say much about yet. What about you?"* Narrator chair, then redirect.
- **The half-invitation** (*"You should come around again sometime,"* vague, costless, status-preserving for them). Match its weight exactly — warm, equally non-committal: *"Yeah, maybe — things are pretty full these days, but I'm sure I'll see you around."* You neither lunged at the door (re-entry seeking) nor slammed it (drama). Their move cost nothing; yours acknowledged that.
- **The public dig** (the joke at your expense in front of an audience — a power-frame display for spectators). The audience isn't judging the line; it's reading your nervous system. Unhurried smile, relaxed tease back — *"Some things never change,"* delivered like you mean it fondly — and carry on. Visible composure flips the see-saw without supplying the conflict they invited. (Counter-attacking hot or appealing to fairness both lose; see §4, Trigger 7 — never fight for status concessions in front of their audience.)
- **The genuine overture** (a real apology or a substantive private reach-out). Hear it. People and groups change; Klaff's prize frame doesn't require permanent enmity, and the decision rule in §6.5 governs. Take it slow, individual, off-territory, and watch behavior over months, not words over minutes.

### 6.5 Elevate past them or cut losses — the decision rule

McCullough's forgiveness research gives a clean two-factor test (Burnette, McCullough, Van Tongeren & Davis, 2012): reconciliation makes sense when **(a) the relationship is genuinely valuable to your future** and **(b) the risk of being exploited or excluded again is low**. Both must hold. Apply it per person, not to the group as a blob — circles contain one or two architects of your exclusion and several bystanders, and the test usually returns different answers for each.

Two systematic biases will corrupt your scoring; correct for both:

- **Sunk cost.** "We have fifteen years of history" is an accounting entry, not a forecast. The sunk-cost effect operates in close relationships — people stay in low-satisfaction bonds because of accumulated investment (Rego, Arantes & Magalhães, 2018; Rusbult's investment model). History is what you're *grieving*, not what you're *deciding about*. Decide on projected future value only.
- **The resignation clock.** Chronic, unresolved ostracism degrades people toward helplessness and depression (Riva et al., 2017). Limbo — orbiting the old circle hoping the weather changes — is not the safe middle option; it is the slow version of the worst outcome. If the two-factor test fails, exit *fully* and build (§7). If it passes for specific individuals, rebuild with those individuals on new ground.

And the reframe to carry out the door: the local-ladder and pond research (§1) means the gatekeepers controlled *one pond's* pricing of you — never your value, and never the supply of ponds.

## 7. Building Your Own Inner Circle

The decisive countermove to gatekeeping is not winning the old circle back; it's becoming the person who *makes* circles. Gatekeepers hold power only over people with one door.

### 7.1 Architecture: the layers

Robin Dunbar's network research describes concentric layers — roughly **5** intimates (the crisis-call people), **15** close friends, **50** regulars, **150** stable contacts — with each layer trading intimacy for reach (the precise numbers are population averages with wide variance; treat them as design guidance, not physics). Engineer deliberately at two layers:

- **The 5** is your board of directors. Selection criteria, derived from the research in this manual: people who pass the **Tesser test** (they can watch you win *in their own domain* without distancing — rare and precious; test it with small wins before trusting them with big ones), people running **benign-envy** physiology (your success makes them push harder, not whisper), and people who tell you the truth at cost. One seat on this board is worth more than the old circle's entire roster.
- **The 50** is your surface area — where new opportunities, introductions, and eventually new members of the 15 come from. It's built by showing up recurringly (see 7.3) rather than by intensity.

### 7.2 Become the broker

Ronald Burt's structural-holes research (Burt, 2004, "Structural Holes and Good Ideas") is the single most useful network finding for a rebuilder: people whose ties **bridge otherwise-disconnected groups** enjoy outsized returns — better ideas, faster advancement, more influence — because they see information early and broadly and become the translation layer between worlds. Socially: don't rebuild one dense clique (a dense clique is just a future gatekeeping opportunity for someone). Build *spokes into several unconnected worlds* — the gym world, the craft world, the old-city world, the scene world — and be the only person standing at the junction. Two properties fall out:

1. **You become structurally ungatekeepable.** No one can push you out of a network whose only common node is you.
2. **You become the introduction machine** — and every introduction is a competitive-altruism deposit (Hardy & Van Vugt, 2006): public, costly-ish generosity that the recipients experience as pure gift and the network records as status.

### 7.3 Host

Hosting is the broker role made flesh, and it's the fastest practical status engine available to a normal person. Priya Parker (*The Art of Gathering*, 2018) names the stance: **generous authority** — the host who runs the room with a confident hand *in service of the guests*: protecting them, connecting them, equalizing them. (Her sharp point: the "chill," hands-off host isn't humble, just abdicating — the room degrades and the guests pay.) Nick Gray (*The 2-Hour Cocktail Party*, 2022 — practitioner source, not research) builds the same case operationally: the regular host becomes "the person everyone wants to know," because they manufacture the social capital everyone else consumes.

Notice what hosting stacks in one move: scarcity's venue control without dominance (your house, your defaults — every beta trap from §5 inverted), competitive altruism with a built-in audience, broker position (you own the junctions you create), and preselection (you're permanently observed at the center of a warm room). Start embarrassingly small — six people, two hours, recurring monthly. Recurrence is the entire trick; the third one is when it becomes an institution and you become its founder.

### 7.4 Value asymmetry and loyalty

- **Give first and publicly** (Hardy & Van Vugt, 2006). Run a surplus with your circle — introductions, real help on their projects, showing up at their things. The status return is empirical, not karmic.
- **Let people invest in you.** The Ben Franklin effect (Jecker & Landy, 1969): people like you *more* after doing you a favor, because they justify the effort by upgrading you. A circle you've only ever given to is a circle with no skin in you. Ask for small, specific things; receive well; mention outcomes.
- **Price commitment in costly signals.** What made the old betrayal cut was that the loyalty was cheap talk. Groups that demand real contribution cohere; the commune-longevity research (Sosis & Bressler, 2003) found communities with *costlier* membership requirements survived dramatically longer. Socially: weight people's *costly* behavior — who drove out, who showed at the hard thing, who defended you in your absence — at one hundred times their warm words, and grant inner-layer access on that ledger only.
- **Don't become them.** The failure mode of the once-excluded is rebuilding the same dominance architecture with themselves at the gate — hoarding ties, punishing members' outside friendships, requiring orbit. That's Case & Maner's threatened-dominator profile, and it produces the same exodus. The prestige architecture is the opposite: be the *connector* whose members are free, whose value is the room itself. People stay loyal to doors that were never locked.

## 8. Countering Money-Flexers

The scenario: average operators using money as their entire status play — picking up checks loudly, logo-wearing, car talk, the implicit *"what do you even drive?"* frame. The research here is unusually one-sided, and it's on your side.

### 8.1 Why the money play is weak

- **The Status Signals Paradox** (Garcia, Weaver & Chen, 2019, *Social Psychological and Personality Science*): people believe luxury displays make them more attractive as potential friends — and they're measurably wrong. Across six studies, observers preferred to befriend the *neutral*-signal person over the BMW-and-Tag-Heuer person. Flexing is a self-defeating strategy executed by people who never checked the receiving end.
- **Status signaling undermines cooperation** (Srna, Barasch & Small, 2022, *JPSP*): people choose *modest* partners when cooperation matters, and savvy operators actively *suppress* their own status signals to be chosen. The flexer is broadcasting on a frequency that repels exactly the relationships he's trying to buy.
- **The framework view** (Cheng et al., 2013; Ridgeway, 2019): money is a *resource*, not status. It converts to status only when an audience's shared values say so. Displayed wealth without skill or generosity is a dominance-adjacent claim — and claims invite audit (Principle 1). The most damning audit question, which everyone silently asks: *if the money is real, why does he need me to see it?* (Note: "flexing signals insecurity" specifically is plausible-but-unproven as a perception finding — see §10. You don't need it; "flexing repels friendship and cooperation" is proven.)
- The **local-ladder effect** (Anderson et al., 2012) is the deep cut: even for the *flexer's own well-being*, face-to-face respect beats socioeconomic display. He's optimizing the metric that matters less, badly.

### 8.2 The counters

**Counter 1 — Refuse the metric (the real fight is frame, not wallet).** A money flex is a frame attack: *the scoreboard here is net worth, and I'm holding the high score.* Compete on it — defend your car, your salary, your spending — and you've ratified the scoreboard while losing on it. The counter is never defense; it's cheerful non-subscription. The flexer announces the new watch: *"That's a good-looking watch"* — full stop, genuine, zero envy, topic moves on. The compliment-without-the-chase is devastating precisely because the flex's entire payout was your visible reaction (attention flows up — §3.3 — and you just declined to flow).

**Counter 2 — The power-buster, when it's an attack rather than a display.** When the money frame turns pointed — *"you should get yourself one of these,"* the loud check-grab engineered for the table — apply Klaff's disrupter: mildly shocking, not unfriendly, wrapped in humor. *"Careful, your watch is wearing you."* / *"You buy the dinners, I'll keep being the reason they're fun."* One beat, grinning, done. It demonstrates — to the audience, which is who the exchange was ever for — that his only weapon doesn't fire on you.

**Counter 3 — Move the venue to your domain.** Klaff's local star power (§5.2): status is situational, and money-flexers are domain-dependent — strip the venue of price tags and many have nothing. Steer the group toward terrain where competence is the visible variable: the trail, the kitchen, the court, the build, the craft. You don't have to beat him at his game; you relocate the game. (This is also why the *analyst* move fails against you in reverse: your status sources — skill, rooms, loyalty — can't be priced, so his scoreboard literally can't render them.)

**Counter 4 — Outspend him in the only currency that buys status.** Competitive altruism (Hardy & Van Vugt, 2006) prices the thing precisely: status goes to costly *contribution*, not costly *consumption*. His $80,000 car bought him nothing at the table; your habit of making introductions, hosting the room, showing up at 2 a.m. — visible, costly, *other-directed* — buys what he thinks he's buying. The deep counter to a money-flexer isn't mocking the money; it's running the generosity ledger he can't, because writing checks is the only generosity he knows and the audience can smell the difference between a gift and a receipt.

**Counter 5 — Modesty as a power move.** The Srna finding cuts both ways: as *you* rise, suppressing your own signals is what the sophisticated do, and audiences know it. The quietest person in the room with the fullest life is a social archetype for a reason. Let your evidence be discovered (inception, §5.2), never presented. Against a flexer, your understatement isn't just taste — it's a contrast frame that does your arguing for you.

## 9. Defenses

You now know the machinery, which means you can see it running on you. This section is the threat catalog: recognition signature → counter.

**The neg / the leveler's joke.** The tease engineered to lower your status in front of the room — Johnstone's see-saw ridden at your expense; Greene's *Laws of Human Nature* "Leveler" type, who uses humor specifically to deflate anyone rising. *Signature:* it lands on your status assets (your win, your standards, your trajectory) rather than your quirks, and arrives with an audience. *Counter:* never visibly bleed (the flinch is the payout — §3.3), never counter-attack hot in front of spectators (§4, Trigger 7). The unbothered laugh-and-tease-back at one notch *below* the incoming heat shows the room you're unhittable without escalating. Pattern-track it: a friend who only levels you when you're up isn't joking; he's filing claims. That's envy data (§6.1), and it should adjust his ledger position (§7.4).

**Gatekeeping.** *Signature:* control of access used as leverage — invitations as rewards, exclusion as punishment, your bonds with other members actively discouraged (the Case & Maner profile: it intensifies the more you grow). *Counter:* the full §6 playbook. The compressed version: never negotiate with the door; build direct lines to the people (§6.3) and build your own rooms (§7.3). Gatekeepers hold power over single-door people only.

**Frame traps.** *Signature:* you keep finding yourself explaining, justifying, auditioning — the interrogated party in conversations you didn't agree to be examined in. Someone is running power, time, or analyst frames on you (§5.1) and you're accepting the witness chair. *Counter:* notice the chair, leave the chair. Stop defending (Principle 7); deploy the matched counter-frame — disrupter for power, your own clock for time, intrigue for analysis. The meta-defense is one habit: *any time you hear yourself justifying unprompted, stop mid-sentence and ask a question instead.*

**Money frames.** *Signature:* conversations that keep arriving at price tags, with you implicitly down-board. *Counter:* §8 — never compete on the metric, relocate the venue, outspend in contribution.

**Status-by-association vampirism.** *Signature:* someone managing you as a prop — warm in private, diminishing in public, your wins repackaged as their mentorship ("taught him everything he knows"), your access shared without reciprocity. The private/public warmth gap is the tell. *Counter:* quietly reprice the relationship to its real terms (acquaintance, not ally), throttle what flows through it, and let costly-signal accounting (§7.4) govern any restoration. No confrontation required — vampires starve on rationed access.

**The misery drag.** *Signature:* the associate for whom every interaction is your energy spent on their permanent grievance, who punishes your good news with deflation. Greene's Law 10 (avoid the unhappy and unlucky) is anecdotal, but emotional-state spillover in networks has at least suggestive research behind it (Christakis & Fowler — contested, see §10), and the opportunity cost is not contested at all: every hour there is an hour not compounding in §7. *Counter:* compassion on a budget. Help concretely where help is usable; exit the subscription where it isn't.

**Your own tells.** The defense audit runs inward too. The four leaks that undo everything in this manual: explaining when no one asked (frame leak), reaching when you should let things come (neediness leak — Klaff's withdrawal discipline), monitoring people who dropped you (attention leak — §3.3, your attention is rank data and you're donating it up the wrong hierarchy), and flexing when you should let evidence be discovered (claim leak — Principle 1). Check quarterly, like smoke detectors. Everyone relapses on at least one.

**When not to play.** The most important defense is knowing where the game *isn't*. Three boundaries, all research-backed:

1. **The 5 don't run on frame.** Inside genuine intimacy, status vigilance is corrosive — the entire value of the inner layer (§7.1) is that it's the one arena where the meter is off, where you can be low-status — confused, scared, wrong — at zero cost. Johnstone is right that transactions never fully stop, but with your 5 the only rule worth enforcing is mutual elevation: both ends of the see-saw, taking turns going up. Run Klaff on your closest people and you'll win frames and end up with clients.
2. **Don't play against the resigned and the desperate** — there's no status in those wins, the audience scores them as bullying (tall-poppy dynamics run in reverse too), and it's dominance behavior, which voluntary circles eventually expel (§1).
3. **Audit the game itself, periodically.** Storr's closing warning is that status games are never finally won — the ladder has no top, and the player is consumed by the climbing. The research in §1 says you can't quit the game; it's hardware. But you choose *which* games, against *whose* scoreboard, in *which* ponds (Principle 9). The end state this manual points at isn't winning the old circle's game. It's building a game where the people you respect hand you status you didn't have to ask for — because of what you contribute, in rooms you built, surrounded by people whose envy of you is the kind that makes them climb. That's prestige architecture. It is slower than every shortcut, and it is the only version that compounds.

## 10. Evidence Quality Notes

This manual cites real research, and honesty about its limits is part of the manual. Items below are flagged either as contested, as inference rather than direct finding, or as practitioner consensus rather than science.

| Claim | Status |
|---|---|
| Power posing changes hormones/behavior | **Effectively debunked.** Failed replication (Ranehill et al., 2015); first author Carney disavowed (2016). Only "may increase *felt* confidence" survives (Cuddy et al., 2018), and even that is contested. Observers reading expansive posture as higher status is a separate, uncontroversial claim. |
| High serotonin = high status (the "lobster" story) | **Contested/oversimplified** in humans. Use "associated with," not "causes." |
| Prestige is more *durable* than dominance | **Reasonable inference** from Cheng et al. (2013) (prestige is liked, dominance isn't), not a measured longitudinal result. |
| "Higher status people react slower" (response latency) | **No direct study located.** Solid adjacent findings: attention flows up-hierarchy (Chance, 1967); composure/vocal control predict rank (Cheng et al., 2016); hesitation reads low-status (Erickson et al., 1978). Treated as inference throughout. |
| Selective availability/scarcity-of-presence as a status signal | **Extrapolation** from scarcity research (Worchel et al., 1975) + costly-signaling theory + practitioner consensus. No direct social-status experiment located. |
| "Flexing signals insecurity" | **Plausible/theoretical, not cleanly demonstrated.** The proven claims are: luxury display deters new friendships (Garcia et al., 2019) and undermines cooperation choices (Srna et al., 2022). |
| "Crab mentality" / "identity anchoring by old friends" | **Folk concepts**, well-*explained* by verified mechanisms (SEM model, malicious envy, social comparison) but not experiments under those names. |
| "Begging for re-entry lowers status" | **Synthesis of two findings** (status-seeking penalty, Mahadevan et al. 2024; reconnection benefits exclude perpetrators, Maner et al. 2007), not a single direct study. |
| "You're the average of your 5 friends" | **Motivational aphorism (Jim Rohn), not science.** Homophily (McPherson et al., 2001) and peer effects are real; the literal math is not. Christakis–Fowler "social contagion" is suggestive but its causal identification is seriously contested. |
| Hotel-towel social proof; watching-eyes effects | Original effects real; **documented non-replications** — context-sensitive, not bulletproof. |
| Milgram obedience studies | Directionally replicated (Burger, 2009) but methodology and the "blind obedience" interpretation are seriously challenged (Perry, 2012; Haslam & Reicher). |
| Mate-choice copying / preselection | **Meta-analytic support, inconsistent individual studies**; effect asymmetric by sex (works for men viewed by women; reverses for women viewed by men — Hill & Buss, 2008). |
| Sunk cost in relationships (Rego et al., 2018) | Sampled **romantic** relationships; friendship extension is reasonable but extrapolated. |
| Powerless-speech and visual-dominance findings | Strongest evidence is from **courtroom/lab contexts**; generalization to friend groups is reasonable but extrapolated. |
| All Robert Greene material | **Anecdotal and amoral by design** — modeled on royal courts, not friendships; Kirkus called the 48 Laws "simply nonsense." Used here only where independently supported (Laws 4, 16, 36) or as named folk strategy. |
| All Oren Klaff material | **Practitioner system, not research** — built for one-shot, high-stakes pitches; critics note its one-dimensional frame-dominance can escalate conflict and damage ongoing relationships. Applied here selectively, with that caveat embedded in §5. |
| Johnstone quotes | Substance well-corroborated across sources; some exact wordings reproduced via secondary sources rather than verified against the 1979 text. |
| General caveat | Most findings come from WEIRD (Western, educated) samples; "universal" claims rest on a literature still skewed toward Western participants. |
